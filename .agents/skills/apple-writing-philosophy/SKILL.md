# Writing Like Apple: A Reasoning Process, Not a Style

## The one first principle everything else derives from

Attention is the scarcest resource the reader has, and every claim has to
survive the reader's skepticism. Apple copy isn't decorated to look a
certain way — it's engineered against those two constraints. Understand
this and you can produce the "Apple sound" for a product Apple never made,
without an example to imitate.

Two consequences follow logically:
- Since attention is scarce, anything that doesn't do direct work is a
  cost, not a neutral addition → short sentences, one idea per unit,
  aggressive cutting.
- Since claims must survive skepticism, vague praise is worthless (anyone
  can say "amazing"), but concrete/falsifiable facts carry weight (a rival
  literally cannot claim a specific number or feature it doesn't have) →
  concreteness, feature-first specificity, letting emotion arrive second.

Everything below is this principle applied to one decision at a time.
Don't memorize rules — rederive them each time by asking: *does this line
respect the reader's attention, and would this claim survive skepticism?*

## The reasoning procedure — run this on your own draft

For each sentence or headline:

1. **Isolate the single idea.** Before writing, state in one clause what
   this line needs to do. Can't state it in one clause? You don't know
   what the line is for yet.
   *Check:* delete everything except that clause — still true and
   complete? If deleting anything changes the meaning, a second idea got
   smuggled in. Split it.

2. **Test every claim for competitor-transferability.** For each claim,
   ask: could a rival honestly say this exact sentence about itself? If
   yes, it's marketing wallpaper — swap the adjective for the specific
   fact (a number, a named capability, a visible outcome) that makes the
   sentence untransferable. This isn't "prefer concrete words as style" —
   it's "abstract claims don't survive scrutiny, so they don't belong."

3. **Decide fact-first or feeling-first.** Default instinct is
   benefit-first. Ask instead: will naming the concrete feature plainly,
   then letting the benefit surface after, make the benefit feel
   *discovered* rather than asserted? If the payoff is genuinely
   surprising once the mechanism is seen, lead with the mechanism and let
   the reader take the last inferential step themselves. If the sentence
   is meaningless without the benefit stated up front, lead with the
   benefit instead. Judge per line — there's no fixed formula.

4. **Read it in one breath.** Run out of air, and some clause is serving
   the writer (hedging, covering yourself) rather than the reader. Cut it.

5. **Only after 1–4, consider rhythm.** Contrast and repetition aren't
   ornamental — they make an already-earned claim easier to hold in
   memory. Adding rhythm to a claim that hasn't passed steps 1–3 uses
   cadence to disguise a weak claim, which defeats the purpose.

6. **Cut, then cut again.** The plain final version is downstream of a
   rougher first draft, not the first draft itself. Write the full
   thought without self-editing, then remove every word not doing direct
   work per steps 1–4. Nothing cut on the second pass usually means the
   first pass under-wrote it.

## Worked example (a product Apple never made)

A backup-battery pack, see-through case, 20-hour capacity.

- **Step 1:** one idea worth leading with — not "durable and stylish and
  long-lasting" (three ideas). Pick the functional promise: capacity.
- **Step 2:** "20 hours of battery" — could a rival with the same spec say
  this? Yes → not yet untransferable. Sharpen toward whatever is actually
  differentiated (an exact mAh figure, a charge count) rather than adding
  unearned flourishes like "rain or shine" unless the product is actually
  weatherproof and rivals aren't.
- **Step 3:** is the raw number more interesting stated first, with the
  feeling ("never think about it again") arriving after? Likely yes — the
  number is unglamorous alone and becomes meaningful once the implication
  lands.
- **Step 4:** read aloud — "10,000 mAh. Three full charges before you even
  remember it needs one." Roughly one breath. Good.
- **Step 5:** a natural contrast exists (see-through case = nothing to
  hide, literally and functionally) — only use it if it survives 1–3 as a
  real claim on its own, not bolted on for effect.

No actual Apple sentence about a battery pack was needed — this is
mechanical application of the reasoning, which is the point.

## The failure mode to actively avoid: mimicry

Signs you've slipped into mimicking rather than applying:
- Reaching for a stock Apple-sounding phrase because it *sounds right*,
  not because this specific claim demanded that structure.
- Adding a short punchy fragment purely for rhythm, with no claim
  underneath that passed steps 1–3.
- Being unable to say which of the six steps produced a given line. If you
  can't trace it back, it was pattern-matched — rewrite from step 1.

## One-line compression

Before every line: *what's the one true, specific thing this needs to
say, would it survive a skeptical competitor hearing it, and have I cut
everything that isn't that?*
